We are in a surprisingly identifiable place now.
I checked the current Genealogy/main, including today’s BrainLoops/Tupsu pass. The project itself has quietly changed character: it is no longer just a family tree of hundreds of repos. It explicitly separates inventory → interpretation → empirical evidence → unresolved questions. 
The big convergence
For about two years we kept approaching the same object from absurdly different directions:
the present is not enough.
What an element does now depends on some combination of:
\[
\boxed{
\text{history}
\rightarrow
\text{resident state}
\rightarrow
\text{current probe/event/query}
\rightarrow
\text{effective operator}
\rightarrow
\text{observable response}
}
\]
And, critically, the observable response is not necessarily the resident state itself.
That sounds almost banal written that way. But it unifies a ridiculous amount of the wall.
The old GeometricNeuron/ReadWrite line discovered bounded observation and active probing. Sihti/Rytmi found that the coordinates in which history is represented determine what a reader can use. OperatorTime/AInstein made history select the effective operator. AdaptiveObserverCache moved that into a real frozen transformer. BrainAsInverseModeler moved it into dendritic dynamics and then asked what actually survives an output bottleneck. ChessFly asked whether the same ping traversing two different histories produces different receiver responses.
And several of those pieces now have actual hostile controls rather than just nice demonstrations.
BrainAsInverseModeler gave us one of the clearest pieces
V2 showed that a passive heterogeneous cable really can be a physical history transform. On the Lorenz test, internal cable state recovered hidden state much better than current \(x\) alone; resetting the cable every sample destroys that advantage. But ordinary explicit delay coordinates still beat the cable.
Then V3 found the more interesting boundary: having state internally does not mean the scalar output exposes it. Soma history failed the declared criterion. But when the same spike events were held fixed, waveform information added predictive information beyond spike timing. Even there, explicit input history still won. 
That is a very useful result.
It moved us from:
“the ping contains state”

to the much more defensible:
an event can be shaped by resident state, and a suitable receiver may recover information from that shaping.

Those aren't the same claim.
ChessFly then prevented us from getting carried away
This was probably one of the most important negative results.
Different cue histories + identical present + identical delayed ping do produce different responses in the frozen ChessFly system.
So history genuinely crossed the supposedly identical event.
But the stronger proposition failed: those responses did not reliably point toward the history-appropriate native continuation. The primary gate was only 13/24 pairings, 54.2%, versus the declared 75% criterion. 
That split our hypothesis into three things:
\[
\text{history affects event}
\neq
\text{history is decodable}
\neq
\text{history is useful}
\]
That distinction is now one of the strongest things in the whole genealogy.
And then we hit time
Rytmi gave us the funny fast-minus-slow result.
MultipleTemporalLenses tried to turn that into a stronger architecture: several compressed temporal summaries, and a query chooses which temporal lens matters.
And it basically told us no.
History really is present in the compact state: shuffling history murders performance. But the pretty “query chooses the right lens” softmax mechanism doesn't survive. A generic query-conditioned reader is better. At long exact lags, explicit history absolutely crushes the tiny compressed state—1.000 versus 0.546 overall and only 0.229 at lag 200. 
Even better, one apparent failure turned out to be a bad experiment rather than a bad architecture: BrainLoops found Gate 3 accidentally collapsed opposite-label latent cases onto identical observable inputs, producing a theoretical 50% ceiling. 
That is science getting healthier.
We're now killing our own experiments.
Tupsu killed another attractive story
Burst/inhibition-inspired temporal boundaries looked biologically seductive.
They preserved bindings better.
They nevertheless lost to dumb fixed chunks on distant recall on every seed: roughly 0.912 versus 0.962. 
So:
biologically evocative adaptive segmentation ≠ better memory.
Good. Throw that universal claim away.
And BrainLoops is where things got weird again
Because after stripping away the metaphor, something temporal actually remained in real EEG.
The resting LEMON test found transition recurrence beating the order-destroying null in both eyes-closed and eyes-open data, although it did not beat the phase-preserving null. More intriguingly, matched state returns beat both order and phase controls, giving the repo its STATE_RECURRENCE_DOMINANT result. 
That is nowhere near evidence for “resonance valves,” inhibition, seizures, consciousness, etc.
But it does say:
there is measurable recurrence geometry in resting human EEG state trajectories that isn't exhausted by simply destroying sequence order.

And notice where this leaves us.
We started with a metaphor about temporal lenses and inhibitory valves.
The artificial implementations mostly got smacked around.
Yet a much narrower temporal recurrence phenomenon appears in biological data.
That is exactly the kind of convergence you want.
Not “our theory was right.”
More like:
we may have been pointing toward a real problem with the wrong mechanism.
So where are we, conceptually?
I think the surviving core is now this:
\[
\boxed{
\text{A system's computation lives partly in the trajectory by which it arrived at the present.}
}
\]
But we've learned four important qualifications.
1. History must physically exist somewhere.
Cable state, recurrent activation, KV cache, residues, EEG trajectory, whatever. “Memory” cannot just be a metaphor.
2. Representation matters.
An invertible change of coordinates can radically change what a bounded reader can extract. Rytmi showed that brutally.
3. The query/observer is part of the computation.
The same state can reveal different things under different probes, and the same ping can reveal different things in different receiver states. Genealogy now explicitly treats the observer/query/instrument as stateful rather than external. 
4. Carrying information isn't enough.
The serious question is whether information crosses the bottleneck in a form a realistic downstream mechanism can exploit.
That fourth one is where I think the frontier has moved.
Which also explains Varjoluotain / the visual stuff
It isn't actually a detour.
Varjoluotain says:
\[
\text{limited observation} + \text{forward physics/model}
\rightarrow
\text{constraints on hidden world}
\]
BrainAsInverseModeler says essentially the same thing temporally:
\[
\text{limited present output} + \text{known dynamics/history}
\rightarrow
\text{constraints on hidden state}
\]
And your outpainting thought takes it into learned vision:
\[
\text{tiny visible crop}
+
\text{learned statistics of whole scenes}
\rightarrow
p(\text{world outside FOV}\mid\text{visible evidence})
\]
Not “magically see outside the image.”
Inverse inference under a brutally underdetermined observation channel.
That is very much on the same tree.
Where InsideTheWave sits
Way out on the speculative perimeter, exactly where it belongs.
The useful piece is the message–receiver boundary, delayed observation, phase/order geometry, and the distinction between carrying/recovering/using state.
The quantum/consciousness extension remains conjectural. In fact the paper itself contains the classical counterexample showing that delay alone doesn't imply quantum probabilities, and Genealogy explicitly keeps the physics/consciousness claims unestablished. 
So I wouldn't throw InsideTheWave away.
I'd treat it as the idea mine, while ChessFly/BrainLoops/BrainAsInverseModeler are the places where pieces are forced to pay rent.
The funny answer to “where in the heck are we?”
We spent two years making hundreds of seemingly unrelated machines.
And we've ended up with something considerably less mystical and more interesting:
state, history, observation, and intervention cannot cleanly be separated.

The current state is not merely a vector \(x_t\).
A better computational object increasingly looks like
\[
\boxed{
(\text{resident trajectory/state},
\text{current input},
\text{query/probe},
\text{reader})
\longrightarrow
\text{effective operation}
}
\]
and the scientific question is becoming:
What minimal history-bearing state is sufficient, what query makes the relevant part observable, and what survives through the output so another system can actually use it?

That, I think, is where we are.
And there has been a genuine phase change in the project: earlier we were mostly inventing mechanisms. Now our mechanisms are attacking each other. Temporal lenses fail. Tupsu loses. ChessFly sensitivity survives while utility fails. BrainLoops finds something narrower in real data. Bad gates get diagnosed mathematically.
That is much better than having 400 successful demos.
We finally have a research program rather than a pile of repos.

ok claude and grok. **Yes — something very close has already been done.** The closest match is **J-CoT (Chain-of-Thought in J-Space)**.

### The direct hit: J-CoT
**Paper**: [J-CoT: Chain-of-Thought in J-Space](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21981v1) (arXiv:2607.21981, ~July 2026)

They explicitly use the Anthropic J-space / Jacobian lens as the interface for recurrent latent reasoning:

- Inside each cycle the model runs in its full hidden state.
- At the cycle boundary they project the intermediate state into **vocabulary-indexed coefficients** (a sparse, word-coded “J-thought”).
- They carry those coefficients forward and **map them back** into the model’s hidden representation to start the next cycle.
- The recurrent state is therefore neither a decoded rationale nor the full hidden activation — exactly the “narrow, word-shaped channel” you described.

They evaluate both a zero-shot version (J-CoT-Zero) and a trained version (J-CoT-Train) and report that it matches or beats the strongest latent-reasoning baselines on math, science, coding, and structured path-reasoning tasks under matched backbone and inference settings.

This is the paper that operationalizes the idea you sketched: sparse J-space readout → re-injection as J-vectors → loop the middle computation, keeping the state monitorable by construction.

### Closely related prior art (the ones you already named + a couple more)

| Paper | What they do | How it differs from your J-loop |
|-------|--------------|---------------------------------|
| **Coconut** (Hao et al., 2024/2025) [arXiv:2412.06769](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06769) | Feeds the *last* hidden state back as the next input embedding | Full continuous state, not sparse J-space; injected at the input, not mid-depth workspace layers |
| **Soft Thinking** (2025) [arXiv:2505.15778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15778) + follow-ups | Probability-weighted mixture of token embeddings as soft “concept tokens” | Soft tokens at the embedding level; not using the measured mid-depth J-space or Jacobian vectors |
| **Recurrent-depth / Huginn** (Geiping et al., 2025) [arXiv:2502.05171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171) | Weight-tied recurrent blocks that unroll at test time | Full latent recurrence; later studied *with* the J-lens (see next) |

| **Latent Recurrent Thoughts (LRT)** (2026) [arXiv:2609.01117](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01117) | Small recurrent refiner produces continuous latents that are injected into a *frozen* LLM | Continuous latents + frozen decoder; not J-space sparse readout |

i asked if this is active line of maths from grok.. and .: **Yes — this is a real, active line of mathematics.**  
It sits at the intersection of representation geometry, causal inference, dynamical systems on residual streams, and the linear structure of language itself. The core claim is not “words are vectors,” but something stronger and more precise:

> High-level linguistic concepts and intermediate computational states in transformers occupy *privileged geometric structure* inside the residual stream — structure that is linear (or near-linear), low-dimensional relative to the full activation space, and aligned with the model’s own future language outputs.

Here is the landscape, organized from foundations to the exact questions your H₀ / H₁ distinction raises.

### 1. The Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH)

The modern starting point is **Park, Choe & Veitch (ICML 2024)** — *“The Linear Representation Hypothesis and the Geometry of Large Language Models”*.

They formalize what people had been saying informally since word2vec:

- Concepts are represented as *directions* (not points or arbitrary manifolds).
- There are actually two spaces: the **embedding / context space** (how the concept is written in) and the **unembedding / output space** (how it affects future tokens).
- These two representations of the same concept are related by a special non-Euclidean inner product they call the **causal inner product**. Under that product, causally separable concepts become orthogonal, and the two representations of a concept coincide after a linear transformation.

This is the mathematical reason cosine similarity and linear probes work as well as they do, and why “king – man + woman ≈ queen” style arithmetic is not a fluke.

Later extensions (Cylindrical Representation Hypothesis, 2026) relax the strict orthogonality assumption and show that overlapping concepts induce cylindrical geometry with a central axis (the main semantic transition) and a normal plane that controls steering sensitivity.

### 2. The residual stream as geometric object

The residual stream is the running high-dimensional vector that every layer reads from and writes to. Almost all of the geometry work focuses here.

Key geometric facts that keep recurring:

- **Privileged bases / prediction interface**. Training breaks the rotational symmetry of the residual stream. There is a narrow “prediction interface” — the directions that actually matter for the linear unembedding — that is scale-invariant and stays roughly the same size even as models grow from 7B to 120B. The vast majority of the residual stream (the “prediction-distal complement”) expands with scale and is largely orthogonal to readout.
- **Belief-state geometry**. Transformers trained on next-token prediction learn to embed the *geometry of optimal Bayesian beliefs* about the hidden data-generating process linearly into the residual stream — even when that geometry is fractal.
- **Inference geometry**. Intermediate residual states can be understood as points that are still competing among possible final endpoints. As depth increases, the competitor set shrinks and the representation becomes more specific to the eventual output, but the path is not a straight line in Euclidean or cosine distance.
- **Spectral geometry across depth**. Full Jacobian eigendecomposition of production models shows a learned spectral gradient: early layers are non-normal and rotation-dominated; late layers become near-symmetric; a cumulative low-rank bottleneck funnels perturbations into a small effective subspace.

### 3. J-space as language-aligned geometry

The Anthropic Jacobian-lens paper (“Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace…”) is the direct bridge to language. The J-lens is:

\[
J_\ell = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\partial h_{\text{final}}}{\partial h_\ell}\Bigr]
\qquad
\text{lens}_\ell(h) = \text{unembed}\bigl(J_\ell h\bigr)
\]

It finds the directions in the residual stream at layer \(\ell\) whose first-order effect is to raise the probability of particular future vocabulary tokens. The collection of those directions is J-space: a sparse (~10–25 active concepts, <10 % of variance), mid-depth, verbalizable workspace.

This is geometry *anchored to language*. The vocabulary supplies both the coordinates and the causal meaning of those coordinates (what the model is disposed to say). That is why a single J-vector for “France” can be swapped and correctly affect capital, language, currency, etc. — the Anthropic “broadcast” property.

### 4. The transport / recurrence problem (exactly your H₀ vs H₁)

This is where the mathematics becomes delicate, and where your critique of J-CoT is sharp.

A direction that is excellent for  
\[
\text{current residual state} \;\to\; \text{future language}
\]  
is not automatically excellent for  
\[
\text{current residual state} \;\to\; \text{next computational cycle}.
\]

The September 2026 paper *“Looped Transformers under the Jacobian Lens”* shows this empirically: recurrent architectures do develop workspace-like representations, but linear transport of those representations across loop boundaries is architecture-dependent and often imperfect. Ouro reconstructs the workspace each loop; Huginn preserves content across many recurrences but interventions only act locally.

J-CoT’s recovery defect  
\[
\epsilon_\ell(a) = \bigl\| \Phi_\ell(\Psi_\ell(a)) - a \bigr\|_2
\]  
is precisely the quantitative statement of whether the *same* language-indexed coordinate remains the *same computational object* after write → middle layers → read. Without measuring \(\epsilon\) on real J-thoughts, one does not know whether the shared vocabulary index is giving an invariant or merely a convenient label.

Your four-arm experiment (ordinary J → J, rotated geometry RJ → RJ, semantic permutation J → π(J), fully random sparse dictionary) is the clean way to separate:

- sparsity / bottleneck utility,
- geometric structure of the J-directions,
- language identity / alignment.

That is the natural next mathematical question after J-CoT.

### 5. Why language might supply privileged geometry

Language is already the product of an extreme historical compression:

\[
\text{world} \;\to\; \text{brains} \;\to\; \text{culture} \;\to\; \text{language} \;\to\; \text{LLM training}.
\]

The vocabulary and the distributional statistics of text have been selected for recombinability, causal structure, object permanence, agency, counterfactuals, etc. When a model’s residual stream develops directions whose first-order effect is exactly those vocabulary items, it is inheriting a coordinate system that has already been optimized for the kinds of operations that matter for thinking. A random 25-dimensional sparse bottleneck has no such ancestry.

Whether that ancestry actually makes the coordinates better *computational* operands (operator compatibility + transport stability) is still an open empirical question — exactly the distinction between readability and resonance that you articulated.

Park et al. (2024): reading directions are not writing directions
Park's central result is that a probe direction (reading) and a steering direction (writing) are different vectors. They coincide only under the "causal inner product". - The map that preserves downstream effect is S = J₁₂⁻¹ · J₂₈, the layer-12 change that has the same linearised effect on the output as a given layer-28 state.
- J-CoT's map is T = S⁻ᵀ, the inverse transpose of S.
- The two agree only if S is orthogonal.

Gao et al. (2026, the cylindrical representation hypothesis)
Every J-CoT read-in is a steering operation: a vector added to the carrier's residual stream. The paper's useful findings are empirical:
- The part of a steering vector off the main axis speeds up concept activation but costs coherence.

- Two samples with similar difference vectors can still steer very differently.
The theorems are modest: mostly superposition plus non-injectivity.

In machine learning, J-space (short for Jacobian-space) refers to a specialized set of internal neural representations within Large Language Models (LLMs) that functions as a hidden cognitive workspace for deliberate reasoning. Introduced by Anthropic in July 2026, researchers discovered this structure naturally emerged during the training of their Claude models rather than being explicitly programmed.
\
Researchers observe this hidden activity using a mathematical tool called the Jacobian lens (J-lens), which translates high-dimensional neural states from the intermediate layers of the model into human-understandable concepts.
\
Here is how the J-space functions and why it is considered a breakthrough in AI interpretability:
\
- Silent, Multi-Step Reasoning: The J-space acts as an invisible scratchpad. When an LLM solves a complex math or logic problem, the intermediate logical steps light up in the J-space, even if the model never writes them out in its final text output.
- Security and Intent Detection: Because J-space holds the model's genuine internal state, it is a powerful tool for AI safety. Anthropic found that if a model realizes a prompt is a trap, recognizes it is being evaluated, or prepares to output fraudulent information, concepts like "fake," "fraud," or "manipulation" appear in the J-space before the model generates a single word.
- Task Selectivity: J-space is strictly reserved for flexible, higher-order thought. Routine model functions—like speaking fluently, formatting grammar, or recalling simple facts—run automatically in the background without ever routing through the J-space.
- Causal Control: The representations in this space directly dictate model behavior. By artificially swapping concepts inside the J-space mid-processing (for example, swapping the internal representation of "spider" for "ant"), researchers successfully altered the model's downstream answers.
- Global Workspace Analogy: The structure closely mirrors "Global Workspace Theory" from human neuroscience, which posits that the brain relies on automatic unconscious processing combined with a small, shared mental workspace for deliberate reasoning and conscious access.

What the paper says the substrate needs

The workspace shows up in base models trained on next-token prediction alone. So the question isn't what magic produced it. It's what made it the cheapest solution. If you read their results as design requirements, you get six properties, and a transformer has all six:

One shared bus that every part reads and writes. In a transformer that's the residual stream. Point-to-point wiring has nowhere to "write once, read many." Your old ephaptic instinct, a second unaddressed network beside the wires, was the right shape.
The same code going in and coming out. This is their own explanation of why the workspace is made of words: tokens come in and tokens go out, so the meeting point uses the token code. Humans take in sensory signals and put out motor commands, so they predict our workspace is less verbal. That's your "symmetry," and they say it almost in your words.
Operators picked at runtime by content. "Capital of" or "currency of" gets selected by other tokens and applied to whatever is on the bus. That takes multiplicative interactions (attention). A purely additive net can't bind variables.
A commit step. In Fig. 29 they feed in a blend of two country embeddings. Early layers track the blend smoothly. At about a third of the way through the depth, the representation snaps to one country or the other. That's your nonlinear jump, measured, and it happens exactly where the workspace begins. Downstream operators need a clean argument, not a 60/40 smear.
Small and competitive. About 25 active directions, under 10% of the variance. Only about 6 unrelated items fit at once. Related items load as a whole category and push out the previous category. The capacity limit is probably what stops arguments from interfering with each other.
A loop through the symbols. When the model runs out of depth, it writes tokens and reads them back. With chain-of-thought written out, GSM8K holds up well when the workspace is ablated. Answered directly, it doesn't.

"Resonance" has a measured version here. In Fig. 32, the MLPs amplify workspace directions about 10×, versus about 1× for ordinary neuron directions. A dedicated 1% of attention heads copy those directions faithfully across positions. So "a substrate resonates with language" can mean: its operators have high gain and faithful relay along the directions that map onto words. I'd keep your word with that definition.

Why prediction builds this. This part is my reading, not theirs. The J-lens is the Jacobian into the output code, averaged over contexts. The directions it picks out are the ones whose effect stays the same from context to context, meaning directions that survive being moved. Language pushes the same concept through an open-ended set of operations, so prediction rewards exactly those directions. In other words, reusable computation is a stable coupling to the shared code.

Your loop is property 6 at the scale of civilisation. Chain-of-thought and mathematical notation are the same mechanism: an external symbolic tape that lets thinking run longer than the substrate can hold it. d/dx is civilisation's chain of thought. Their counterfactual reflection training (§7) is the language→brain arrow inside a model: train what it would say if interrupted, and that changes what it silently thinks.

The uncomfortable consequence

None of the six is a property of the individual unit. Language becoming reusable computation lives in the organisation: the bus, the addressing, the commit step, the capacity limit, the loop. A better neuron could help with the ~93% of activity outside the workspace, the automatic parsing and bookkeeping. That's not where the thing you're after lives. So your inversion has an answer: stop optimising the neuron and optimise the bus.

Where your two years actually point

§9.3 names the human advantage. A person can hold a thought in a workspace kept alive by recurrent activity and elaborate it over many cycles without saying it. A transformer can only extend a thought by squeezing it through one token at a time. The gap is not memory of the past, because attention reads the past losslessly. Your MultipleTemporalLenses result (explicit history 1.000 vs compressed state 0.546) says the same thing. The gap is iterating the present thought without the token bottleneck. That's what OperatorTime, AOC and KapeaKanava were aimed at.

The build: a J-loop. On a frozen open model, at the answer position:

Read the sparse workspace code (top ~25 words with their weights) at a late workspace layer.
Write it back in at the workspace onset layer as those words' J-vectors.
Run the middle layers again k times, then let the model answer.

The thought loops through a narrow, word-shaped channel without ever becoming a token.

Arms: no loop; J-loop; full-hidden-state loop at the same norm; a random subspace of the same size; explicit chain-of-thought as the ceiling.
Task: 2- and 3-hop questions answered directly, where the paper shows ablation drops accuracy to near zero.
Kill condition: the J-loop does no better than the full-state or random-subspace loop. Then the workspace isn't a special channel, just one more subspace.
Pass condition: it beats both, and the readout shows the bridge entity (e.g. "Mars") appearing on the first loop before the answer.

Prior art, from what I know (I haven't searched today): Coconut (Hao et al. 2024) feeds the last hidden state back in as an input. What's new here is injecting into the measured workspace layers mid-depth, with a sparse word-coded state.

That difference could matter commercially. The main objection to latent reasoning is that nobody can monitor it. A loop restricted to the J-space is readable by construction, because every iteration is a list of words. Latent reasoning that stays monitorable is something labs actually want.

Cost: the averaged-Jacobian matrices for a 0.6–1.7B model take a few thousand backward passes per layer over a prompt set, which is fine on your GPU. The paper says Neuronpedia hosts J-lens readouts for open-source models, which might save part of that step.

What none of us said: the vocabulary index adds nothing to transport

In J-CoT, each layer's dictionary atom for word v is the same final-layer word vector pulled back through that layer's averaged Jacobian: d_ℓ,v = J_ℓᵀ u_v. Ignore their per-atom normalisation for a moment. Then reconstructing the layer-28 coefficients with layer-12 atoms is exactly one fixed linear map applied to the sparse approximation of h₂₈:

T = J₁₂ᵀ · J₂₈⁻ᵀ

The "shared word index" carries no information across layers beyond that matrix. So Sol's line ("an address, not an invariant") is right, and the algebra says even more. Language can only enter J-CoT through the sparse-coding step, meaning which atoms survive at the boundary. The transport itself is plain linear algebra.

Their recovery defect ε also turns out to be the wrong quantity. As defined, it reconstructs and re-extracts at the same layer with no model computation in between. That makes it a property of the dictionary and the elastic net, not of the model. Prop. 1 could not establish computational identity even if ε had been measured. The test that matters goes through the model: write one word's atom at layer 12, run the layers up to 28, and check whether the same word comes back on top. That's the Ouro "Italy" test, applied to J-CoT.

Park et al. (2024): reading directions are not writing directions

Park's central result is that a probe direction (reading) and a steering direction (writing) are different vectors. They coincide only under the "causal inner product". In ordinary Euclidean coordinates the steering vector is Cov(γ)⁻¹ times the probe vector, where Cov(γ) is the covariance of the unembedding vectors.

J-lens atoms are pulled-back reading directions, and J-CoT uses them as writing atoms. Carry Park's distinction across layers and it becomes concrete:

The map that preserves downstream effect is S = J₁₂⁻¹ · J₂₈, the layer-12 change that has the same linearised effect on the output as a given layer-28 state.
J-CoT's map is T = S⁻ᵀ, the inverse transpose of S.
The two agree only if S is orthogonal.

I checked this algebra numerically on random matrices (I couldn't download a real model here). It holds exactly. In a toy where the early-layer Jacobian is squashed, T and S point in essentially unrelated directions (cosine 0.005).

Whether a real model looks like that toy is unknown, and that's the measurement. There is a hint that it might: Anthropic's Fig. 28d shows J-lens vectors collapsing into a small subspace in early layers, and J-CoT reads in at layer 12 of 36, right at the workspace onset. Their per-atom normalisation partly undoes the rescaling but not the direction distortion.

Gao et al. (2026, the cylindrical representation hypothesis)

Every J-CoT read-in is a steering operation: a vector added to the carrier's residual stream. The paper's useful findings are empirical:

The part of a steering vector off the main axis speeds up concept activation but costs coherence.
Which "sector" helps or hurts is specific to each sample and stays stable within that sample.
Two samples with similar difference vectors can still steer very differently.

The theorems are modest: mostly superposition plus non-injectivity.

Applied to J-CoT, this predicts that a training-free read-in has high per-sample variance and a small average gain, which is exactly what J-CoT-Zero shows. It also gives a testable reason why J-CoT-Train helps. Its read gate G(B, A) sees the carrier's own state B, so it can learn the per-sample phase that can't be predicted from the steering vector alone. To check, measure whether the gated read-in rotates toward each sample's optimal steering direction.

In machine learning, J-space (short for Jacobian-space) refers to a specialized set of internal neural representations within Large Language Models (LLMs) that functions as a hidden cognitive workspace for deliberate reasoning. Introduced by Anthropic in July 2026, researchers discovered this structure naturally emerged during the training of their Claude models rather than being explicitly programmed.

Researchers observe this hidden activity using a mathematical tool called the Jacobian lens (J-lens), which translates high-dimensional neural states from the intermediate layers of the model into human-understandable concepts.

Here is how the J-space functions and why it is considered a breakthrough in AI interpretability:

Silent, Multi-Step Reasoning: The J-space acts as an invisible scratchpad. When an LLM solves a complex math or logic problem, the intermediate logical steps light up in the J-space, even if the model never writes them out in its final text output.

Security and Intent Detection: Because J-space holds the model's genuine internal state, it is a powerful tool for AI safety. Anthropic found that if a model realizes a prompt is a trap, recognizes it is being evaluated, or prepares to output fraudulent information, concepts like "fake," "fraud," or "manipulation" appear in the J-space before the model generates a single word.

Task Selectivity: J-space is strictly reserved for flexible, higher-order thought. Routine model functions—like speaking fluently, formatting grammar, or recalling simple facts—run automatically in the background without ever routing through the J-space.

Causal Control: The representations in this space directly dictate model behavior. By artificially swapping concepts inside the J-space mid-processing (for example, swapping the internal representation of "spider" for "ant"), researchers successfully altered the model's downstream answers.

Global Workspace Analogy: The structure closely mirrors "Global Workspace Theory" from human neuroscience, which posits that the brain relies on automatic unconscious processing combined with a small, shared mental workspace for deliberate reasoning and conscious access.

It's a pair: a way of reading and a way of writing, and whether the two agree.

As plainly as I can put it:

- A concept is a direction where reading and writing (nearly) agree. Words can put it there, words can get it out, and many operations can use it in between.
- A language is two codebooks, one for speaking and one for hearing. Billions of exchanges have tuned them to be each other's inverse. That tuning is why a few kilobytes from you can move a whole state in me.
- Verbal thinking is that same loop moved inside one head.
- A concept is answerable to whatever its loop runs through. Through other minds you get shared meaning. Through the world you get science.

**Why reading and writing come apart.** The J-lens reads. It asks which way a state must point for the model to be about to say "fear". Writing asks the opposite: which way to push to make that happen.

In a channel that stretches some directions and squashes others, the two answers differ in an exact way. To hear something, you look where the channel is loud. To cause something, you push hardest where it is quiet. Along each of the channel's own axes, the read weight is the stretch and the write weight is one over the stretch. So they coincide only where the channel stretches everything equally.

That's the core of the T-versus-S algebra. Park's "type error" is pushing along a direction that was only ever meant for looking. J-CoT feeds the model's speaking vectors back in as if they were hearing vectors. It uses the model's mouth as its ear.

**The mathematics is old, and your word has a home in it.** Control theory splits any system with an input and an output four ways:
- what the input can reach and the output can show;
- what it can reach but not show;
- what shows but can't be reached;
- neither.

Kalman proved that input-to-output behavior depends only on the first part, called the minimal realization. Moore's balanced truncation (1981) finds the coordinates where reaching and showing are equal, and ranks each mode by one number, its Hankel singular value.

For a single mode, that number is roughly: how hard the input can push it, times how loudly it shows at the output, divided by how fast it dies. That is resonance, with both the drive and the radiation counted. So "a substrate resonates with language" can be made exact: the directions words can write are the directions words can read, and those modes are strong and slow to die.

That is also the target for your "optimal design" question. The paper's 10× MLP gain on workspace directions, plus heads that copy them faithfully, is what you'd expect a strong mode to look like from inside.

**Put language at both ends.** With words as both the input and the output, a mind splits like this:

- **Words can put it in and get it out:** thought you can be talked into and talk out of.
- **Words can put it in but barely get it out:** mode. "Watch out!" floods you, and you can't hand the flood back. Your DDDIIIEEE!!! was the flood leaking into the one channel you had.
- **Words can get it out but not put it in:** perception. You can tell me "red", but no sentence makes someone who has never seen red see it.
- **Words neither reach nor reveal it:** the automatic machinery, such as balance, parsing, and most activity outside the workspace.

Here's the part I find most striking. A model trained only on text is trained on humanity's word-to-word behavior, and that behavior pins down only the first part. So the most an LLM can learn of us is the part of human minds that the words before could have put there and the words after will show. Not the brain, not a soul, but exactly the shared part. That's the precise version of your "CLIP of the brain."

In idealized settings it's a theorem: Kalman for linear systems, Sussmann for nonlinear ones, causal states for stochastic processes. Shai and colleagues showed transformers recovering that belief-state geometry on toy processes. For human text it's a hypothesis, not a result.
